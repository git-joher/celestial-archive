Arsenal of the God of War

Green Dragon Crescent Blade

The 82-jin blade that carved a dynasty. The crimson horse that crossed a thousand li. The armor of a warrior saint.

Quick Answer

Guan Yu's arsenal centers on the legendary Green Dragon Crescent Blade (青龙偃月刀, Qīnglóng Yǎnyuè Dāo) — a massive guandao polearm said to weigh 82 jin (approximately 41–49 kg). It is named for the crescent-shaped blade and the green dragon engraved along its spine. He rode the incomparable warhorse Red Hare (赤兔马, Chìtù Mǎ), a crimson steed capable of traveling a thousand li in a single day. His iconic green battle robes, bronze armor, and the Spring and Autumn Annals he read by candlelight complete the image of a warrior-saint whose weapons were as legendary as the man who wielded them.

The Green Dragon Crescent Blade 青龙偃月刀

Picture the scene: a late-night camp in the wilderness of the Central Plains. The fires of the Shu Han army flicker in the darkness. Inside a general's tent, a single candle burns. Seated on a wooden stool, the nine-foot-tall warrior Guan Yu reads from a worn bamboo scroll — the Spring and Autumn Annals, the Confucian chronicle of ancient states. His long black beard, which he is said to have meticulously groomed every morning, falls over the pages. His phoenix eyes, narrow and penetrating, trace the characters by the trembling flame. And resting against the wall beside him, catching the candlelight like a frozen stroke of lightning, stands his blade.

The Green Dragon Crescent Blade (青龙偃月刀) is not merely a weapon in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms — it is a character in its own right. Forged from refined steel and celestial iron, the blade rises nearly six feet from a dragon-carved wooden shaft before curving into a broad, sweeping arc reminiscent of a crescent moon. Along the spine of the blade, a green dragon is said to have been engraved in such exquisite detail that it appeared to coil and breathe whenever the blade caught the light. The dragon was more than ornamentation: it was a symbol of Guan Yu's righteous fury, the embodiment of the force that cut down traitors and carved the path of Shu Han.

The weight of the blade is given in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms as 82 jin. To understand what this means, one must first understand the jin. In the Han dynasty, one jin was approximately 220 grams — making the blade roughly 18 kilograms (about 40 pounds) in historical terms. However, later dynasties recalibrated the jin to approximately 596 grams in the Ming dynasty (the era when the Romance was written), which would give the blade a staggering weight of nearly 49 kilograms — about 108 pounds. The Romance's author, Luo Guanzhong, likely used the Ming-era jin, meaning Guan Yu was said to swing a weapon equivalent to a small adult human in battle, with one hand, from horseback. Even at the lighter Han-dynasty interpretation of 18 kilograms, the blade was an extraordinarily heavy weapon that would have required tremendous strength to wield effectively.

The crescent shape was not arbitrary. The sweeping curve of the blade allowed for devastating slashing attacks from horseback — a rider charging at full speed could let the blade's momentum carry it through multiple enemies in a single pass. The inner curve could hook shields, disarm opponents, and pull riders from their saddles. The flat of the blade, wielded with enough force, could crush armor without even using the edge. It was a weapon designed for a singular purpose: one warrior, on horseback, cutting through the enemy ranks like a scythe through wheat. The green dragon engraving was said to possess its own spiritual power, with some accounts claiming that the blade would emit a low hum before battle, as if the dragon within sensed the coming conflict.

In the world of Chinese mythological weapons, the Green Dragon Crescent Blade occupies a unique place — it is entirely mortal in origin yet has achieved the same legendary status as magical weapons like Sun Wukong's Ruyi Jingu Bang or Erlang Shen's three-pointed spear. Unlike those divine armaments, the Green Dragon Crescent Blade had no celestial forging, no magic spells, no ability to shrink or grow. Its power was simply that Guan Yu wielded it. This groundedness makes it perhaps the most human of all great Chinese weapons — a testament to mortal strength, skill, and willpower rather than divine intervention.

To compare: Sun Wukong's Ruyi Jingu Bang, weighing 13,500 jin (about 8 tons), was originally a pillar holding up the Eastern Sea, forged by Yu the Great to measure the depths of the floodwaters. It could shrink to the size of a needle concealed in Wukong's ear or expand to pierce the very heavens. Erlang Shen's three-pointed spear, the 三尖两刃刀 (sān jiān liǎng rèn dāo), was a divine weapon gifted by the heavens, forged from celestial jade and capable of splitting mountains. Both are clearly supernatural. But the Green Dragon Crescent Blade has no such magical pedigree — it was forged by human hands, yet through Guan Yu's legendary feats, it became every bit as famous as its divine counterparts. In the hierarchy of Chinese mythological weapons, the blade stands as proof that mortal greatness can rival the divine.

Could It Really Exist? 偃月刀的历史真实性

The question of whether the Green Dragon Crescent Blade truly existed has occupied historians and martial arts enthusiasts for centuries. The guandao — the general class of polearm to which the Green Dragon Crescent Blade belongs — takes its very name from Guan Yu (the character "关" in guandao is his surname). By tradition, the weapon type was named after him, which would suggest that if the guandao exists, it was named in honor of Guan Yu's signature weapon.

Historical evidence tells a more complicated story. The earliest known depictions of guandao-like weapons in Chinese art date from the Song dynasty (960–1279 CE), several centuries after Guan Yu's death in 220 CE. Many historians argue that the guandao as a distinct weapon type did not exist during the Three Kingdoms period. The Romance of the Three Kingdoms, written in the 14th century during the Ming dynasty, likely retroactively equipped its heroes with the weapons familiar to its own age, much as Hollywood sets medieval films with anachronistic armor from later periods.

Furthermore, the weight of 82 jin (in Ming-era measurement, roughly 49 kg) presents practical problems. A weapon of that weight would be extraordinarily difficult to wield effectively in combat. Modern replicas of the Green Dragon Crescent Blade used in martial arts demonstrations typically weigh between 5 and 15 kilograms — a heavy weapon, certainly, but far below the legendary 49 kg. At 49 kg, the blade would be better classified as a lifting implement than a combat weapon. It would have been impossible to swing from horseback without unbalancing both rider and horse. Even at the Han-dynasty interpretation of 18 kg, the blade would have been extremely heavy — the weapon of a truly exceptional warrior, but not superhumanly so.

Yet the myth persists — and perhaps that is the point. The weight of the blade is not a historical claim but a literary one. It tells us that Guan Yu's strength was legendary, that he belonged to a different order of being from ordinary men. The blade's reported weight is a narrative device that signals to the reader: this is no ordinary general. The historical debate may never be settled, but the cultural truth is undeniable. The Green Dragon Crescent Blade exists in the space between history and legend, and that liminal quality is precisely what makes it so powerful as a symbol.

Red Hare 赤兔马

No account of Guan Yu's arsenal is complete without the Red Hare (赤兔马, Chìtù Mǎ) — the legendary crimson warhorse that was as much a part of his legend as the Green Dragon Crescent Blade itself. The horse's name literally means "Red Rabbit," a reference to its speed (the idea being that it was as swift as a rabbit, but with the size and power of a full-grown warhorse). Its coat was said to be the color of cinnabar, a deep and brilliant red that made it unmistakable on the battlefield. It stood fifteen hands high, with a muscular build and a temper to match.

The Red Hare was famous before it ever carried Guan Yu into battle. Originally the mount of the warlord Lü Bu — the most feared warrior of the early Three Kingdoms period — the horse was so legendary that a popular saying spread through the land: "Among men, Lü Bu; among horses, Red Hare" (人中吕布，马中赤兔). Lü Bu rode Red Hare to countless victories, his speed advantage allowing him to dictate the terms of every engagement. When Lü Bu was defeated and executed in 199 CE, the horse passed into the hands of Cao Cao, the great warlord of the Wei kingdom.

Cao Cao, ever the pragmatist and manipulator of men, understood the value of both horse and loyalty. When Guan Yu was temporarily under Cao Cao's service — this was after the fall of Xiapi and before Guan Yu's journey to rejoin Liu Bei — Cao Cao made a calculated decision. He presented Red Hare to Guan Yu as a gift, hoping to bind the great general to his cause. But Guan Yu, in a moment that perfectly encapsulates his character, thanked Cao Cao and said: "I know that this horse can travel a thousand li in a day. Now that I have him, if I ever hear word of my elder brother Liu Bei, I will be able to reach him in a single day." The implication was clear: the gift of the most famous horse in China would not sway Guan Yu's loyalty. It would instead help him return to his oath-brother all the faster.

And indeed, when Guan Yu learned that Liu Bei was alive and in the south, he mounted Red Hare and rode out — the famous "passing through five passes and slaying six generals" (过五关斩六将) journey that is one of the most celebrated episodes in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms. Red Hare carried him through enemy territory, across rivers and mountains, outrunning every pursuer.

The horse's loyalty to Guan Yu became as legendary as the blade. According to historical and literary accounts, when Guan Yu was captured and executed by Sun Quan's forces in 220 CE, Red Hare refused to eat. Day after day, the great crimson stallion stood in silence, refusing grain and water. Within days, it died of starvation — choosing death over service to any other master. Whether historically accurate or not, the story of Red Hare's death serves as a perfect parallel to Guan Yu's own unwavering loyalty. If Guan Yu was the embodiment of righteousness, Red Hare was the embodiment of that same virtue in animal form — a creature that would rather starve than serve a master it did not choose.

Armor of the Warrior Saint 武圣铠甲

Guan Yu's iconic appearance is as recognizable as his blade. In every statue, painting, and film adaptation, he wears a specific combination of armor and robes that has become the definitive visual representation of the God of War. The image is so standardized that it has remained essentially unchanged for over a thousand years.

Over his armor, Guan Yu wears green battle robes (绿袍) made of heavy silk brocade, embroidered with golden thread in patterns of clouds and dragons. The green — the color of pine, the color of the forests of his homeland in Xie County (modern-day Yuncheng, Shanxi) — became his signature. In Chinese color symbolism, green represents vitality, growth, and harmony. For Guan Yu, it also served a practical purpose: on the battlefield, the green robes against the brown earth and gray sky made him an unmistakable figure to his troops, a beacon around which they could rally.

Beneath the robes, he wears bronze scale armor (青铜甲) that covers his torso and shoulders. The scales overlap like the tiles of a temple roof, each one individually riveted to a leather backing. The bronze has a dark patina, aged by countless battles. Over his chest, a round mirror-polished plate catches the sun — a Taoist protective talisman meant to deflect evil spirits and enemy arrows alike. His shoulders are covered by layered pauldrons in the shape of dragon heads, their mouths open in silent roars.

His headdress is equally distinctive. Guan Yu is almost always depicted wearing a green silk headwrap (包巾) that covers his hair and is tied at the back, with two trailing ends that fall behind his shoulders. On top of this wrap, he wears a golden headband (抹额) with a single ornament in the center — often a gem or a small dragon motif. This headwrap-and-headband combination distinguishes him from the crowned emperors and helmeted generals around him, marking him as something between a gentleman-warrior and a folk hero.

Perhaps the most important element of his appearance, however, is his face. Guan Yu's face is described as the color of a "dark date" (重枣, zhòng zǎo) — a deep, rich red. This has been variously interpreted as a description of a naturally ruddy complexion, a sign of his righteous anger made permanent, or a symbolic device to mark him as a figure of transcendence. In Chinese opera, the red face denotes loyalty, courage, and uprightness — the same qualities embodied by Guan Yu himself. His phoenix eyes (丹凤眼) are long and narrow, slanting slightly upward at the corners, giving him an expression of perpetual stern judgment. His beard is famously described as "two feet long, as beautiful as silk" — a magnificent black beard that reaches to his chest, which he maintained with meticulous care.

Spring and Autumn Annals 春秋

Perhaps the most unexpected item in Guan Yu's arsenal is a book. The Spring and Autumn Annals (春秋, Chūnqiū) is a historical chronicle of the State of Lu covering the period from 722 to 481 BCE, traditionally attributed to Confucius himself. It records events in terse, dry entries — "In the tenth month, the Earl of Zheng sent his son to the court of Lu" — but in Confucian tradition, every word was believed to carry profound moral judgment.

Guan Yu's habit of reading the Spring and Autumn Annals by candlelight during military campaigns is one of the most cherished details of his legend. According to the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, even during the chaos of war, Guan Yu would retreat to his tent at night, light a single candle, and lose himself in the ancient text. He is often depicted in art with the scroll open before him, his blade resting nearby, the candle casting long shadows across his red face and flowing beard. The juxtaposition is striking: the warrior who could cut down a hundred men sits reading the moral lessons of antiquity.

This detail transforms Guan Yu from a simple warrior into a scholar-warrior (儒将, rú jiàng), a figure who embodies the Confucian ideal of the cultivated gentleman. In traditional Chinese thought, the highest ideal was not the pure warrior or the pure scholar but the man who combined both — who could wield the sword in defense of civilization and the brush in its cultivation. Guan Yu represents this ideal perhaps better than any other figure in Chinese history. His devotion to the Spring and Autumn Annals is not mere antiquarianism; it is the source of his moral compass. The Annals taught him about loyalty, about righteousness, about the proper order of human relationships. When he made decisions — to serve Liu Bei, to spare Cao Cao, to refuse Sun Quan's offers of alliance — he was acting on principles learned from the ancient text.

Some versions of the legend go further, claiming that Guan Yu did not merely read the Annals but annotated them, writing his own commentaries in the margins between battles. Whether this is true or apocryphal is almost irrelevant — it speaks to a deeper cultural truth. The God of War is also the God of Literature in some traditions, and his temples often double as places of study and examination. The blade and the book are two sides of the same coin: one cuts down enemies, the other cultivates the soul. Together, they make Guan Yu not just a god of war but a god of righteous order.

The Weapon in Popular Culture 流行文化中的青龙偃月刀

Few historical weapons have achieved the cross-cultural recognition of the Green Dragon Crescent Blade. It has appeared in virtually every video game, film, and television adaptation of the Three Kingdoms story, and its distinctive silhouette is instantly recognizable even to those who have never read the Romance. Here is how the blade — and Guan Yu's broader arsenal — appears across modern media.

Video Games

In Dynasty Warriors, Koei's long-running hack-and-slash series, Guan Yu appears as a playable character in every installment, always wielding the Green Dragon Crescent Blade as his signature weapon. His moveset emphasizes wide, sweeping arcs that can clear entire battalions of enemy soldiers in a single swing — a fitting digital representation of the legendary blade's power. The weapon has appeared as a special unlockable item across the entire Warriors franchise, from the mainline Dynasty Warriors games to crossover titles like Warriors Orochi.

In Honor of Kings (王者荣耀), the massively popular mobile MOBA by Tencent, Guan Yu is a playable champion who charges into battle on horseback — a unique mounted mechanic that sets him apart from nearly every other character in the game. His blade swings in massive arcs, and his ultimate ability summons a stampede of spectral cavalry.

In Smite, the third-person MOBA, Guan Yu fights as a playable god wielding the Green Dragon Crescent Blade, with his abilities drawing on both the weapon and Red Hare. His in-game model faithfully reproduces the iconic green robes and red face, and his taunts reference the Spring and Autumn Annals.

Film and Television

In the 2008 epic film Red Cliff (赤壁), directed by John Woo, Guan Yu is portrayed as a formidable warrior wielding a heavy guandao — the film took care to show the weapon as a physically demanding implement, with the actor training for months to achieve believable blade movements. In the 2010 Chinese television series Three Kingdoms (三国), Guan Yu's blade was crafted as a specially weighted prop, and the actor's training with it became a focus of the production's promotional material. The 1994 series Romance of the Three Kingdoms (三国演义) featured perhaps the most iconic television portrayal of Guan Yu, with actor Lu Shuming's solemn, dignified performance setting the standard for all subsequent portrayals.

Statues and Folk Art

Every Guan Yu temple and statue around the world — from the massive 80-meter-tall statue in Yuncheng, Shanxi to the smallest household shrine — includes the Green Dragon Crescent Blade as an essential element. The blade is not just a weapon but an icon of Guan Yu's identity, as inseparable from him as the cross is from Christ. In Chinese folk art, the blade is often depicted with the dragon clearly visible along the spine, coiling as if alive, and the blade itself is sometimes shown with a golden glow, emphasizing its semi-divine status.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much did the Green Dragon Crescent Blade really weigh?

In the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, the blade is said to weigh 82 jin. Depending on the dynasty's measurement system, this translates to approximately 18–20 kg using Han dynasty jin (about 220 g each), or up to 49 kg using Ming dynasty jin (about 596 g each). Most historians and martial arts experts believe the Han-dynasty equivalent of roughly 18–20 kg is the more historically plausible weight, though even that would have required exceptional strength. Modern martial arts replicas typically weigh between 5 and 15 kg, which is considered heavy but manageable for trained practitioners.

What happened to Red Hare after Guan Yu died?

According to the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, Red Hare was captured by Sun Quan's forces following Guan Yu's execution in 220 CE. The horse was given to Ma Zhong, the general who had captured Guan Yu. However, Red Hare refused to eat, starving itself to death within days. This story is widely regarded as a literary invention — a symbolic parallel to Guan Yu's own unwavering loyalty. In historical records, the ultimate fate of Red Hare is not recorded, though the horse would have been quite old by the time of Guan Yu's death, as it had previously served Lü Bu in the 190s CE, making it at least 25–30 years old by 220 CE.

Is the guandao a real martial arts weapon?

Yes, the guandao is a real weapon practiced in traditional Chinese martial arts, particularly in styles that emphasize long pole weapons. The name "guandao" (关刀) literally means "Guan's blade," named after Guan Yu. However, most historians believe the guandao shape did not exist during the Three Kingdoms period and emerged later, possibly during the Tang or Song dynasties. The weapon is featured in many wushu forms and demonstrations, and modern practitioners train with versions weighing between 5 and 15 kg. The guandao's distinctive crescent-shaped blade makes it one of the most visually striking weapons in the Chinese martial arts repertoire.
