From Villain to Icon

The Bull's Eternal Legacy

How the defeated demon king became one of the most beloved characters in Chinese mythology — reimagined across centuries of opera, film, television, and the boundless imagination of popular culture.

The Enduring Legacy of the Bull Demon King in Chinese Culture

I
Classical Reception

From Page to Stage

The Bull Demon King has been a fixture of Chinese opera for centuries, particularly in Peking Opera and regional forms like Sichuan Opera. The Flaming Mountain episode, with its spectacular battle scenes, dramatic transformations, and the show-stopping reveal of the colossal white bull, makes for thrilling theater. In opera adaptations, the Bull Demon King is typically portrayed with elaborate bull-head makeup and costume — a role that demands both martial arts skill and dramatic presence. The "Flaming Mountain" episode became one of the most frequently performed Journey to the West excerpts, alongside "Havoc in Heaven" and "The White Bone Demon." In these performances, the Bull Demon King is rarely a pure villain — he is a tragic figure, a king defending his home against an invading force that happens to be the heroes of the story. Opera audiences, familiar with the full context of the brotherhood and betrayal, often sympathize with him.

II
Television and Film

The Bull on Screen

The Bull Demon King has appeared in virtually every major adaptation of Journey to the West. In the beloved 1986 CCTV television series — the definitive adaptation watched by billions — the Flaming Mountain arc is one of the most memorable storylines, with the Bull Demon King portrayed as a sympathetic antagonist whose rage is rooted in justified grievance. Stephen Chow's 2013 film Journey to the West: Conquering the Demons features a radically reimagined Bull Demon King as the film's primary antagonist. The 2014 fantasy epic The Monkey King starring Donnie Yen as Wukong features the Bull Demon King as a major character, played with gravel-voiced intensity. In anime and animation, the Bull Demon King appears in the classic 1960s Japanese series Saiyuki, the beloved Dragon Ball franchise (as Gyūmaō, the Ox-King), and numerous Chinese animated productions. Each adaptation reinterprets him — sometimes as pure villain, sometimes as honorable warrior, sometimes as comic relief — but his presence is constant. No adaptation of Journey to the West is complete without him.

III
Video Games

The Bull King as Interactive Icon

In video games, the Bull Demon King has found a new dimension of popularity. In Smite, the MOBA game featuring mythological figures from around the world, the Bull Demon King is a playable character — reimagined with a design that blends Chinese iconography with modern fantasy aesthetics. In Honor of Kings (王者荣耀), China's most popular mobile game, the Bull Demon King is a playable tank hero who transforms into his white bull form for his ultimate ability. In Black Myth: Wukong (2024), the groundbreaking Chinese action RPG, the Bull Demon King appears as a major character — and true to the novel, he is portrayed with depth and moral complexity, not as a simple boss to defeat. Perhaps most creatively, the mobile game Journey to the West: Bull Demon King centers the character as its protagonist, telling the Flaming Mountain story from the demon king's perspective — a narrative choice that reflects how the character's moral ambiguity has made him increasingly compelling to modern audiences.

IV
Folk Hero Status

From Demon to Antihero

In contemporary Chinese popular culture, the Bull Demon King has undergone a remarkable transformation: from obstacle to be overcome to antihero to be rooted for. This shift reflects broader cultural changes in how audiences relate to mythological figures. Modern readers are less likely to accept the celestial hierarchy's authority at face value; the Bull Demon King's refusal to submit resonates with anti-authoritarian sensibilities. His role as a husband and father — however flawed — makes him more relatable than the celibate monk or the enlightened Buddha. His defeat, which the original novel frames as justice, reads to modern audiences as tragic: a sovereign defending his home against an empire that will not tolerate independence. On Chinese social media, the Bull Demon King is frequently meme-ed as a relatable figure — the stressed-out patriarch, the betrayed brother, the king who just wants to be left alone. His status as one of Chinese mythology's most beloved "villains" is a testament to the complexity Wu Cheng'en wrote into him five centuries ago.

V
The Bull in World Culture

Beyond Chinese Borders

The Bull Demon King has traveled far beyond China. In Japan, he appears as Gyūmaō (牛魔王) in the Dragon Ball franchise, where he is reimagined as the Ox-King — a powerful but ultimately good-hearted figure who is Chi-Chi's father and Goku's father-in-law. This adaptation, while far removed from the original, has introduced the archetype of the bull demon king to global audiences numbering in the hundreds of millions. In Western fantasy, the Bull Demon King has influenced the design of bovine demon lords and minotaur kings, becoming part of the global vocabulary of mythological creatures. The 2024 release of Black Myth: Wukong brought the Bull Demon King to millions of Western players for the first time, presented with a visual and narrative sophistication that honored the character's Chinese origins. As Journey to the West continues to be translated, adapted, and reimagined for global audiences, the Bull Demon King stands alongside Sun Wukong, Nezha, and Erlang Shen as one of the characters who transcends cultural boundaries — a figure whose power, pride, and tragedy speak a universal language.

VI
The Meaning

Why the Bull Endures

What makes the Bull Demon King endure, across centuries and media, is that he represents something fundamentally human beneath his demon exterior. He is not motivated by abstract evil or cosmic ambition — he wants to protect his family, rule his home, and be left in peace. These are profoundly relatable desires, and his tragedy is that the system he lives in will not allow them. He is the demon who dared to build something — not just a lair, but a life. And when heaven came to take it apart, he fought back not out of ideology but out of love, pride, and rage — the most human of motivations. In a mythology filled with divine bureaucrats, enlightened Buddhas, and reformed demons who accept their place in the hierarchy, the Bull Demon King stands apart: the one who said no, the one who was not reformed, the one whose defeat was a loss not just for himself but for the possibility of true independence. That is why audiences, century after century, keep coming back to him. He is the demon who was more human than the gods.
